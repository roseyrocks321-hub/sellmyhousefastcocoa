Rising Costs and Tax Uncertainty: Why Cocoa Landlords Are Selling Before 2027

Brevard County's perfect storm of property taxes, insurance hikes, and ballot uncertainty

Home → Blog → Rising Costs Landlords Cocoa

Owning rental property in Cocoa, FL used to feel like a safe bet. You bought a duplex near the historic district, a single-family home in Rockledge, or a small apartment building in Merritt Island, and you expected steady cash flow, tax advantages, and long-term appreciation. But 2026 has been different. The ground has shifted beneath Brevard County landlords, and many are discovering that the math no longer works.

In September, Brevard County commissioners adopted a 2026-27 budget of roughly $2.43 billion and an aggregate countywide millage rate of 4.7491 mills. While the total budget is actually 6.3% smaller than last year's $2.59 billion, local landlords know the truth: their property tax bills keep climbing, their insurance premiums have jumped again, and a major ballot measure in November could rewrite the property tax landscape entirely. If you are a rental property owner in Cocoa, Titusville, Rockledge, or anywhere along the Space Coast, you are probably doing the same math your neighbors are — and wondering whether it is time to sell.

This guide breaks down the forces squeezing Brevard County landlords right now, what the November 2026 ballot means for your property taxes, and how to exit landlording quickly without losing your equity to repairs, vacancies, and market uncertainty.

The Perfect Storm Hitting Brevard County Landlords

Landlords in Florida have always dealt with hurricanes, humidity, and seasonal tenant turnover. But 2026 has layered new pressures on top of those familiar challenges. Three forces in particular are pushing rental owners toward burnout.

Property Taxes Are Climbing Again

Brevard County's final property tax rate for 2026-27 sits at 4.7491 mills, approved at the September 22 public hearing. While commissioners point out that the overall budget shrank, individual property owners are seeing their tax bills rise because assessed home values across the county have increased substantially over the past several years. For landlords who bought properties in Cocoa or Merritt Island before the recent run-up, those higher assessments translate directly into heavier annual tax burdens — often with no corresponding increase in rental income.

Compounding the issue, Brevard County commissioners have indicated they may need to override a voter-approved tax cap to fund critical infrastructure needs. That means future tax bills could climb even higher, regardless of what happens to property values. For landlords already operating on thin margins, this uncertainty makes long-term planning nearly impossible.

Amendment 3 Looms Over the November Ballot

Florida voters will decide Amendment 3 on November 3, 2026, and the outcome could substantially change taxable property values beginning in 2027. While the amendment did not cause Brevard County's current budget pressures — the county's 2026-27 tax rate was set independently — its passage would create a new financial reality for every property owner in the state.

For landlords, the uncertainty is paralyzing. Should you raise rents now to cover potential tax shifts? Should you refinance before lending standards tighten? Should you hold through 2027 and hope the amendment fails, or sell now while buyer demand in Cocoa and Rockledge remains relatively strong? Every option carries risk, and the clock is ticking toward Election Day.

Insurance Costs Continue to Spiral

Florida's property insurance crisis shows no sign of easing in 2026. Landlords across Brevard County report premium increases of 30% to 80% over the past two renewal cycles, particularly for older properties near the Indian River Lagoon or in low-lying areas of Cocoa and Titusville. Many insurers have stopped writing new policies in coastal Florida altogether, forcing rental owners into the state-backed Citizens Property Insurance Corporation or smaller surplus-line carriers with higher deductibles and stricter coverage limits.

For a landlord with a $250,000 rental house in Cocoa, a jump from $3,200 to $5,800 in annual insurance premium is not unusual. That extra $2,600 per year — more than $200 per month — wipes out the profit margin on many single-family rentals. When you add property taxes, maintenance, property management fees, and the inevitable vacancy months, the investment that looked brilliant in 2019 looks like a liability in 2026.

What Rising Costs Look Like for Cocoa Rental Owners

Theory is one thing. Reality is another. Here is what the new economics of landlording look like for rental owners in Cocoa, Rockledge, Merritt Island, and Titusville in late 2026.

The Math Stops Working

Consider a typical three-bedroom rental house in Cocoa with a market rent of $2,100 per month. At first glance, that sounds like solid income. But strip away the actual costs:

  • Mortgage payment (PITI): $1,400-$1,600 per month, depending on purchase price and rate
  • Property taxes: $250-$350 per month, and climbing with new assessments
  • Insurance: $300-$500 per month for properties in flood-prone or wind zones
  • Maintenance and repairs: $150-$300 per month averaged annually
  • Property management: $150-$210 per month if outsourced
  • Vacancy reserve: $100-$200 per month to cover turnover gaps

Add those figures together and a landlord can easily face $2,350 to $2,960 in monthly expenses against $2,100 in rent. Even without a mortgage, the combination of taxes, insurance, and upkeep consumes most of the gross rent. The property may still appreciate over time, but the monthly cash flow is negative — and that is before an air conditioner fails, a tenant moves out unexpectedly, or the roof needs replacing after a tropical storm.

Aging Housing Stock Means Constant Repairs

Much of Cocoa's rental inventory was built between the 1960s and 1980s. Homes in the College Green area, near Saturn Road, and along Dixon Boulevard have charm and solid construction, but they also have aging plumbing, outdated electrical panels, original windows, and flat or low-slope roofs that demand constant attention. After Hurricane Milton and other 2024-2025 storms, many landlords discovered their insurance deductibles had risen so high that they paid for repairs out of pocket.

Every $3,000 repair is three months of profit gone. Every $8,000 roof patch is a year of cash flow evaporated. For landlords who bought these properties expecting passive income, the reality is a part-time job managing contractors, inspectors, and tenant complaints — usually during evenings and weekends.

Why Local Landlords Are Especially Vulnerable

Brevard County landlords face pressures that rental owners in inland Florida simply do not experience. The local economy, geography, and tenant base create unique vulnerabilities.

Military Tenant Turnover Near Patrick Space Force Base

Patrick Space Force Base and Cape Canaveral Space Force Station employ thousands of military members, civilian contractors, and support staff. Many landlords in Cocoa, Satellite Beach, and Merritt Island rely on military tenants with housing allowances who pay reliably and respect property. But military life comes with Permanent Change of Station (PCS) orders, often with 30 to 60 days' notice. A landlord who finally found a great tenant in March may receive notice in October that the family is transferring to Colorado.

That turnover creates predictable but exhausting cycles of cleaning, advertising, showing, screening, and onboarding new tenants. In a softening rental market — which some Brevard County property managers are beginning to report — those vacancies last longer and require rent concessions to fill. A free month of rent, which was unheard of in 2022, is now offered on some Cocoa and Rockledge listings just to attract qualified applicants.

Seasonal and Vacation Rental Competition

Cocoa Beach and the surrounding coastline draw tourists year-round, and many property owners have converted long-term rentals into short-term vacation units. That shift reduces the available long-term housing stock, but it also increases competition for the landlords who still rent by the year. Tenants with good credit and stable jobs can now choose from renovated condos with pool access and modern appliances — amenities that older Cocoa rental homes simply cannot match without major capital investment.

The landlords left holding older single-family rentals find themselves competing on price rather than quality, which squeezes margins even further. Lowering rent to $1,850 to attract a tenant may fill the unit, but it also guarantees negative monthly cash flow once taxes and insurance are paid.

Your Exit Options as a Tired Landlord

If you are reading this and recognizing your own situation, you have several paths out of landlording. Each has trade-offs.

  • List with a real estate agent: This can maximize sale price in a strong market, but it requires repairs, cleaning, staging, and months of showings. You also pay 5-6% in commissions plus closing costs. If your property needs work or has tenants who complicate showings, this path is slow and expensive.
  • Sell by owner (FSBO): You avoid agent commissions, but you handle all marketing, showings, negotiations, and legal paperwork yourself. Most FSBO sales take longer and sell for less than agent-listed properties.
  • Raise rents and hold: Some landlords try to pass cost increases to tenants. In Brevard County, rent growth has slowed in 2026, and aggressive increases risk vacancies or tenant turnover that costs more than the raise generates.
  • Sell to a cash buyer: A local cash home buyer can purchase your rental property as-is, with tenants in place or vacant, and close in 10 to 14 days. You avoid repairs, agent fees, open houses, and months of uncertainty. The offer is typically below full retail market value, but when you factor in saved commissions, repair costs, and months of carrying expenses, the net difference is often smaller than landlords expect.

For landlords who are truly burned out — the ones who lie awake worrying about the next repair bill or tenant issue — the speed and certainty of a cash sale often outweigh the theoretical top price of a traditional listing.
